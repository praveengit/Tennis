# Tennis Racket Inspection Knowledge Base

Reference guide used when analyzing a photo of a tennis racket. Each section
lists what can be seen in a photo, what it means, and what to recommend.
Some checks (sound, feel, tension) cannot be done from a photo; in those cases
tell the player how to check it themselves. Sources are listed in
`knowledge/SOURCES.md`.

## 1. Strings

### 1.1 Visible signs the strings need replacing
- **Broken string**: restring now. Playing on a broken bed puts uneven load on
  the frame.
- **Notching**: grooves or flat spots worn into the main strings where they
  cross the cross strings. Caused by mains sawing against crosses, mostly with
  topspin. Deep notches lock the string bed, kill snapback and spin, and mean a
  break is close. This is the most important visual sign for polyester.
- **Fraying / fuzz**: fibres sticking out of multifilament, synthetic gut or
  natural gut. Looks worse than notching but has less effect on play. Heavy
  fraying around the sweet spot means a break is near.
- **Strings out of alignment**: mains shifted sideways and staying out of line
  (visible as uneven gaps in the pattern). Means the strings have lost
  resilience/tension and should be replaced.
- **Discolouration, dull or chalky look**: old strings. Not proof on its own,
  but supports other signs. Coloured polyester fades and goes dull with use.
- **String savers** (small plastic pieces at crossings): show the player breaks
  strings often; fine to keep, they extend string life.

### 1.2 Strings can be dead without visible damage
- All strings lose tension from the moment they are strung: a large drop in
  the first minutes/hits, then about 10-15% in the first 24 hours for
  polyester, and it keeps dropping whether the racket is used or not.
- Polyester loses tension fastest, nylon/multifilament less, natural gut least.
- Polyester is usually "dead" after roughly 8-15 hours of hard hitting (some
  sources say 10-20) while still looking intact. Multifilament / synthetic gut
  last about 20-40 hours.
- "Dead" means more than lost tension: the strings stop sliding and snapping
  back, so spin and control drop.
- Symptoms the player feels: less control (balls flying long), less spin,
  dull "thud" instead of a "ping", having to push strings back into place
  between points, new arm discomfort.

### 1.3 How often to restring
- Rule of thumb: restring as many times per year as you play per week
  (3 times a week = 3 restrings a year). Restring at least once a season even
  if you play little, because strings age on the frame.
- This rule is too slow for polyester, heavy topspin or competitive play:
  polyester every 8-15 hours of hard play.
- Rough guide: playing several times a week = every 1-2 months; occasional
  play = every 3-4 months.
- If the player says the strings are 6+ months old, recommend a restring even
  if the photo looks fine.

### 1.4 String types (identify from photo where possible)
- **Polyester / co-poly**: single solid strand, smooth or shaped (angular) or
  textured surface, often coloured (black, yellow, red, blue, grey). Durable,
  controlled, spin-friendly, but stiff and loses tension quickly. Hard on arms.
- **Multifilament**: many thin fibres wrapped together; often clear, white or
  natural colour; frays and fuzzes with wear. Soft, comfortable, powerful.
- **Synthetic gut**: solid nylon core with wraps; cheap, all-round; frays.
- **Natural gut**: tan / honey colour, most comfortable and holds tension best;
  frays and is sensitive to moisture.
- **Hybrid**: mains and crosses visibly different (often poly mains with
  multifilament or gut crosses).

### 1.5 Tension guidance
- Most frames print a recommended range on the inside of the throat or on the
  shaft (often roughly 45-65 lbs). Start in the middle of that range and adjust
  by 2-3 lbs.
- Lower tension = more power, comfort and a larger sweet spot.
  Higher tension = more control, less power, harsher feel.
- Typical starting points: beginners about 48-54 lbs, intermediate about
  52-57 lbs, advanced about 55-62 lbs.
- String polyester about 10% lower than you would string a multifilament.

## 2. Arm comfort (tennis elbow)
- If the player reports elbow, wrist or shoulder pain, recommend:
  - A softer string: multifilament or natural gut instead of polyester. If they
    want poly, a soft co-poly at a lower tension (about 48-52 lbs).
  - Dropping tension by about 3-8 lbs.
  - Replacing dead polyester promptly; dead poly gets stiffer and transmits
    more shock.
  - Checking grip size; a wrong grip size makes you squeeze harder.
- A vibration dampener changes the sound and feel of the string bed but has
  not been shown to reduce shock reaching the arm. Do not recommend it as an
  elbow fix.

## 3. Frame

### 3.1 Cracks
- A cracked frame should not be played with: cracks get worse with every hit,
  change how the racket plays, and can fail suddenly. Most cracks cannot be
  repaired reliably; recommend replacing the racket.
- Most common crack locations: the **throat** (where the frame splits into two
  arms), the **head at 10 and 2 o'clock**, the **3 and 9 o'clock** sides, and
  under the **bumper guard** or inside the **grommet strip**.
- In a photo, a crack looks like a thin line that runs across the frame
  (often perpendicular to the frame's length), may continue through paint and
  graphite, may have slightly raised edges, or shows the frame surface
  separating. A paint chip is a surface patch with irregular edges and no line
  continuing into the frame.
- Paint cracks in the throat of older frames can be just paint, because the
  frame flexes there. If unsure, say so and ask the player to check.
- Checks the player can do (not visible in a photo):
  - **Tap test**: tap around the frame with a knuckle or coin; a healthy frame
    makes a clear consistent "ping", a crack makes a dull thud or buzz.
  - **Touch**: run fingers along the frame for ridges, soft spots or dips.
  - **Remove the bumper guard** to look underneath.
  - **Flex**: gentle pressure; one side giving more than the other is a bad
    sign.
  - On court: sudden loss of power, more vibration, a hollow "dead" feel.
- Ask a stringer to check the frame at every restring.

### 3.2 Scratches, chips and paint damage
- Scratches and paint chips at the top of the head (from scraping the ground)
  are cosmetic, but deep scuffs that expose bare graphite need protection:
  replace the bumper guard or add head-protection tape.

### 3.3 Warping and heat damage
- Heat (for example a hot car boot) can loosen strings and warp the frame;
  around 60 °C can distort a frame. A warped head looks asymmetric or not
  flat when viewed edge-on. Store rackets at room temperature (about 15-20 °C).
- Damp storage can cause mould on grips and degrade strings.

### 3.4 Frame age
- Graphite frames slowly lose stiffness over years of impacts.
- Club player playing 2+ times a week: best performance for about 2-3 years,
  replacement typically somewhere between 3 and 10 years. Hard-hitting
  tournament players: about 1 year.
- Old paint schemes or models discontinued long ago can hint at an old frame;
  mention it only as a possibility.

## 4. Bumper guard and grommets
- The **bumper guard** is the plastic strip around the top of the head; the
  **grommets** are the plastic tubes the strings go through.
- Replace when:
  - The bumper guard is worn through and bare graphite shows at the top.
  - Grommets are split, cracked, flattened, missing, or have sharp edges, so
    string can touch the frame.
  - Roughly every 50-60 hours of play or every third restring on a frequently
    used racket, or every 1-2 years.
- Not replacing them causes faster string breakage (string rubbing the frame
  or sharp edges) and can damage the frame.
- Replacement sets are model-specific; do it at the next restring.

## 5. Grip and overgrip
- Overgrip signs of wear: discoloured (white/pink going grey or dark), shiny
  and smooth, frayed or torn, flaking, peeling at the top, slippery even after
  cleaning, or looks dirty/stained.
- Replace overgrip about every 1-2 months for 1-2 sessions a week, every 3-4
  weeks for 3-5 sessions a week, weekly for competitive players.
- The base (replacement) grip under it also wears; replace it when it is
  flattened, torn, or the bevels (edges of the handle) can't be felt.
- Grip size: measure from the middle palm crease to the tip of the ring
  finger, or hold an eastern forehand grip and check that the index finger of
  the other hand just fits between fingertips and palm. Too big makes wrist
  action harder and can contribute to tennis elbow; too small makes you
  squeeze. Grip size can be built up with an overgrip or heat-shrink sleeve,
  not reduced much.
- Grip size usually can't be judged from a photo; suggest the check if the
  player mentions slipping, squeezing or arm pain.

## 6. Accessories and customisation
- **Vibration dampener**: personal preference for sound/feel. Missing
  dampener is not a problem. A cracked or torn one can be replaced cheaply.
- **Lead tape**: adds weight for stability and power. At 3 and 9 o'clock it
  adds stability on off-centre hits; at 12 o'clock it makes the racket more
  head heavy (power on serves); under the grip it makes it more head light
  (more manoeuvrable). Peeling or loose lead tape should be re-secured or
  replaced.
- **String savers**: see 1.1.

## 7. String pattern
- Written as mains x crosses, e.g. 16x19 (open) or 18x20 (dense).
- Count from a clear, face-on photo when possible; otherwise say it could not
  be counted.
- Open patterns (16x19, 16x18): more string movement, more spin and power,
  strings wear and notch faster.
- Dense patterns (18x20): more control, flatter trajectory, strings last
  longer, less forgiving.
- Swing technique and string condition affect spin more than pattern.

## 8. Analysing a photo: checklist
1. Is it a tennis racket? (Not badminton, squash, padel or pickleball.)
2. Identify racket brand/model from paint and text if readable; note the
   printed tension range if visible.
3. Strings: broken? notched? frayed? shifted out of line? string type and
   colour? hybrid? string savers?
4. Frame: lines that look like cracks (throat, 10/2, 3/9 o'clock, near
   bumper), deep chips, bare graphite, warping.
5. Bumper guard and grommets: worn through, split, missing.
6. Grip/overgrip: colour change, shine, tears, peeling.
7. Accessories: dampener, lead tape, string savers.
8. Combine with player info (level, style, frequency, last restrung, pain)
   for setup advice: string type, tension, restring timing.
9. Be honest about limits: if the image is blurry, at an angle, or only shows
   part of the racket, say what could not be checked and how to take a better
   photo (whole racket face-on in good light, plus close-ups of strings at the
   centre, the throat, the top of the head and the grip).

## 9. Priority guide
- **High**: broken string; visible or suspected crack; grommets letting
  string touch the frame; deep notching; strings older than 6 months for a
  regular player; arm pain with stiff/dead poly.
- **Medium**: worn-out overgrip; bumper guard worn through; strings shifted out
  of line or past their recommended hours; tension/string type mismatch for the
  player.
- **Low**: cosmetic scratches and paint chips; dampener choice; optional
  customisation such as lead tape.
